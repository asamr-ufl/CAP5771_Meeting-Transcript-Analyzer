WHAT WE WORKED ON THIS WEEK
-----------------------------------------------------------------------

This week we built the visualization dashboard notebook and wrapped up
Milestone 2. The notebook is data_visualization_static.ipynb and
contains both static panels and interactive widget extensions.

STATIC DASHBOARD PANELS
We organized the static section into six panels that tell a complete
story about meeting efficiency:

Panel 1 — KPI cards at the top give the viewer five headline numbers
instantly: total meetings, average efficiency score, average decision
density, average redundancy, and average action clarity. These orient
the viewer before any charts appear.

Panel 2 — An efficiency score histogram shows the distribution is
roughly bell-shaped and slightly left-skewed, meaning most meetings
cluster around moderate efficiency with a tail of very inefficient
meetings. A cluster pie chart next to it shows about half of all
meetings fall into the two lower-efficiency categories.

Panel 3 — KDE curves split by cluster show how the four efficiency
groups differ on each individual metric. The most striking finding
here is that redundancy score separates Highly Efficient meetings from
all other clusters more cleanly than any other metric. High-efficiency
meetings have noticeably lower redundancy — they cover ground once
and move on. Decision density is also higher but the separation is
less dramatic.

Panel 4 — An interactive cluster explorer widget lets the viewer
filter to any cluster and see per-meeting bar charts for each metric,
plus a table of the top and bottom 5 meetings by efficiency score.

Panel 5 — A per-meeting deep dive widget lets the viewer select a
single meeting and see its utterance timeline (colored by label),
label breakdown bar chart, metric scorecard, and sample decision and
action utterances. This is useful for understanding what the numbers
mean in terms of actual language.

Panel 6 — A model performance summary shows F1 per class and a box
plot of efficiency scores per cluster, confirming that the cluster
labels are well-separated on the target metric.

WIDGET EXTENSIONS
Beyond the two widgets built into the static section (cluster explorer

- W1: Side-by-side meeting comparison with delta annotations
- W2: Label filter and transcript browser with a formatted utterance table

- W4: Speaker contribution explorer with utterance/word count toggle

WHAT WE LEARNED FROM THE VISUALIZATIONS
The main finding is that redundancy is the dominant signal for meeting
inefficiency. Many meetings in the AMI corpus revisit the same topics
repeatedly without making decisions. Action items, when they do appear,
often lack explicit ownership — the action clarity score is low even in
moderately efficient meetings.

This has a practical implication for the Milestone 3 Streamlit app:
the most useful alert we can surface for a user is not "your meeting
had few decisions" (which they probably already sense) but rather
"40% of your utterances were repetitive" — that is the insight that
is hardest to notice in real-time.

SETBACKS
-----------------------------------------------------------------------

The per-meeting deep dive widget initially crashed on meetings with
very few utterances because the word count KDE plot requires at least
two data points. We added a try/except guard around the KDE call with
a fallback to a simple bar chart.

We also had an issue with the ipywidgets Output context manager not
clearing correctly when switching meetings rapidly — solved by using
clear_output(wait=True) which defers clearing until new content is
ready, eliminating the flicker.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------
REFLECTION ON MILESTONE 2 AS A WHOLE
-----------------------------------------------------------------------

Looking back at the five weeks of Milestone 2, the biggest lesson
is that feature engineering took far longer than modeling. The decision
about how to measure redundancy and what threshold to use involved a
lot of manual inspection and iteration. The actual model training, by
comparison, was fast once the features were ready.

The class imbalance problem is real and we handled it explicitly at
every stage — in the labeling step (keyword rules that look for
specific positive signals rather than assuming everything is
discussion), in the classifier training (class_weight='balanced'), and
in the evaluation metric (macro-F1 rather than accuracy).

If we were starting over, we would have written the validation checks
earlier — before feature engineering, not after. We caught a small bug
in the redundancy calculation during validation that would have silently
corrupted downstream models if we had not checked.
